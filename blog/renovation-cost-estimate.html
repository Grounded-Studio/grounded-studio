How to Create an Accurate Cost Estimate for a Renovation

📅 June 14, 2026 ⏱ 6 min read ✍️ Grounded Studio

Going over budget is the number one source of stress on renovation projects. It's rarely because materials cost more than expected — it's because the estimate was built on guesswork rather than a structured process. A proper cost estimate, done before a single tool is picked up, is the difference between a smooth project and a painful one.

Professional builders and contractors use a clear methodology to price every job. It's not complicated, but it is thorough. Here's the full step-by-step approach you can use for any renovation, from a bathroom refresh to a full floor-to-ceiling overhaul.

Step 1: Break the project into tasks

Start by listing every single thing that needs to happen from start to finish. Don't group things together — be granular. A "bathroom renovation" is not a task. "Remove existing tiles", "waterproof the wet area", "install new tiles", "fit new sanitaryware" and "paint the walls" are tasks.

Work through the project chronologically: demolition and preparation first, structural work second, first-fix trades (electrics, plumbing), then materials installation, then finishing (skirting, painting, cleaning). This forces you to think through every stage rather than skipping something that will turn into a surprise cost later.

A good task list for even a medium renovation will have 15–30 line items. If yours has five, you're almost certainly missing something.

Step 2: Calculate material quantities for each task

For each task that involves materials, calculate exactly how much you need. This is not the stage for rough guesses — precision here directly determines your budget accuracy. Use proper calculations: floor area for flooring, wall area for tiles and paint, linear metres for skirting and coving.

Where possible, use a calculator tool to eliminate arithmetic errors. Small mistakes in quantities compound into large cost overruns once you multiply by unit price and then again by the number of tasks.

📐 Measure twice, buy once

Errors in quantity estimates don't just cost money — they cost time. Getting an order wrong means waiting for a redelivery, potentially halting work on site. Take your measurements carefully, write them down, and check them before calculating. A few extra minutes at the measurement stage saves hours of disruption later.

Step 3: Get actual material prices

Once you know exactly what quantities you need, get real prices. Contact your supplier or check their website. Don't estimate based on what you vaguely remember things costing — prices shift, and a 20% error on materials is very easy to make without checking.

Include delivery costs in your calculation. On a large order, delivery can be a flat fee that's easy to absorb. On several small orders from different suppliers, delivery charges add up fast.

Material cost per item
Material cost = Quantity × Unit price
Example: 7 boxes of tiles at €34.95 per box = €244.65

Do this for every single material line item: tiles, adhesive, grout, flooring, underlay, paint, primer, screws, filler, tape, sealant — everything. The small consumables are where budgets quietly leak (see common mistakes below).

Step 4: Add labour costs

Labour is typically the largest cost on any renovation. There are two scenarios here: you're doing the work yourself, or you're hiring contractors. If you're doing it yourself, assign a realistic time estimate to each task and decide whether that time has an opportunity cost for you. If you're hiring out, you need quotes.

For contractor work, day rates vary significantly by trade and region. The table below gives approximate ranges as a starting reference point — always verify with local quotes for your area:

These are broad approximations. For regulated trades like electrical and plumbing, always get at least three quotes from licensed contractors. Prices vary enormously based on complexity, location and how busy the trade is at the time. Never accept a single quote as the market price.

Step 5: Add a contingency buffer

Unexpected things always come up on renovation projects. A hidden pipe where you didn't expect one. Structural issues revealed once the old plasterboard comes down. A tile that gets cracked in delivery. These aren't failures of planning — they're the nature of working with existing buildings. Budget for them in advance rather than being caught short.

For a straightforward cosmetic renovation in a property you know well, 10% contingency is usually adequate. For older buildings, anything involving structural work, or projects with significant unknowns, use 15% or even 20%.

Final estimate with contingency
Final estimate = (Materials + Labour) × 1.15
Example: (€1,200 materials + €2,400 labour) × 1.15 = €4,140

Present your estimate to yourself — or your client — as two numbers: the base estimate and the contingency total. This is more transparent than hiding the contingency inside the line items, and it makes it easier to track where the budget is going as the project progresses.

Common mistakes that blow renovation budgets

Even experienced renovators make these errors. Being aware of them in advance means you're far less likely to repeat them:

  • Forgetting small consumables. Screws, wall plugs, filler, masking tape, sandpaper, adhesive, grout, silicone sealant, primer — none of these are expensive individually, but across a whole renovation they can easily add up to €150–300 that was never accounted for. Include them as a line item.
  • Underestimating labour time. First-time renovators consistently underestimate how long tasks take. Prep work alone — removing old materials, fixing surfaces, cleaning up — can take as long as the visible work. If you're doing it yourself, be honest about this.
  • Not getting quotes. Assuming a contractor will cost "around €X" without a real quote is a direct route to a nasty surprise. Get quotes in writing, specifying exactly what is and isn't included in the price.
  • Ignoring permit and delivery costs. Building permits, skip hire for waste, scaffolding if needed, and multiple delivery charges all sit outside the usual materials and labour calculation. List them explicitly.
  • Skipping the contingency. "We'll figure it out if something comes up" is not a plan. The contingency buffer exists precisely because unexpected things always come up. Don't omit it.
