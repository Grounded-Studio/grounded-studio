How to Save €400 a Year on Food Waste (Without Changing Your Diet)

📅 July 27, 2026 ⏱ 6 min read ✍️ Grounded Studio

I built FreshGarant because of one uncomfortable number: the average person in the Netherlands throws away 33.4 kg of edible food per year. For a two-person household that's roughly €400 straight into the bin — every single year. That's a decent holiday, a new laptop, or roughly 8 weeks of groceries. Silently wasted.

The interesting part: nobody wastes food on purpose. It happens because life is busy, fridges are messy, and by the time you spot that forgotten yoghurt at the back, it's already gone. Below is exactly where the €400 goes, and six habits — five of them completely free — that will keep most of it in your wallet.

Where the €400 actually goes

According to the Dutch Voedingscentrum (National Nutrition Centre), five product groups make up over 60% of household food waste:

  • Bread — the number one wasted item. Half loaves that go stale before the weekend.
  • Dairy — yoghurt, quark, cream, and milk pushed past the "best before" and thrown out untouched.
  • Fresh vegetables — spinach, lettuce, herbs and courgettes that wilt in the crisper drawer.
  • Potatoes — bought in bulk, forgotten, sprouted, binned.
  • Cooked leftovers — the "I'll eat that tomorrow" plate that sits in the fridge for a week.

Notice a pattern? These are all things you already bought. You paid for them. The waste isn't happening at the checkout — it's happening at home, after the money's already gone.

Habit 01 — Free

Do a 30-second fridge check before you shop

Open your fridge and freezer. Look at what's about to expire. Build your meal plan around that, not around what a recipe blog told you to make. This single habit cuts most people's waste by 20–30% in the first month. Zero effort, zero cost, immediate results.

Habit 02 — Free

Store bread properly (or freeze it)

Bread is the #1 waste category, and it doesn't have to be. Two options: (1) buy a smaller loaf more often, or (2) slice it and freeze half straight away. Frozen bread toasts perfectly from frozen — no defrosting needed. You'll never bin half a loaf again.

Habit 03 — Free

Learn the "best before" vs "use by" trick

Best before = quality date. Milk, yoghurt, cheese, eggs, sauces — usually fine days or weeks after this date. Smell it, taste a bit. Use by = safety date, mostly on raw meat/fish. Take that one seriously. Most people conflate the two and throw away perfectly good food. Full explainer here.

Habit 04 — Free

The "leftover night" rule

One night a week — usually Thursday — no new cooking. You eat what's already in the fridge. It sounds boring; it's actually liberating. You save money, you save time, and Sunday's roast doesn't become Wednesday's science experiment.

Habit 05 — Free

FIFO your fridge like a supermarket

When you unpack shopping, put the new stuff at the back and pull the older stuff to the front. Supermarkets do this so shelves rotate. Do the same at home and things stop getting forgotten in the corners.

Realistic math: what €400 actually buys you

Let's put that €400 in perspective. Per year, it's about €33 per month. Some of that is baked into every big grocery run — the yoghurt you didn't finish, the herbs that wilted, the bread heel that hardened. Even shaving off 60% of the waste means €20 back in your pocket every month. That's a takeaway pizza a week, a Netflix subscription, or a decent bottle of wine on Friday night. Same food budget. Just less of it ending up in the compost bin.

And there's the environmental angle: 33 kg of avoided waste per person = about 92 kg of CO₂ per year not emitted, plus 25,000 litres of water not spent growing food nobody eats. That's not marketing puff — it's the same math the Voedingscentrum uses in their annual reports.

The one-week test

Don't take my word for it. Do this for seven days: every time you throw food away, write down what it was and roughly what it cost. That's it. No apps, no habits changed. By day 7 you'll have your personal number — and it'll probably surprise you. Then decide whether it's worth changing anything.

Most people are shocked. That's usually enough to trigger habit #1.
