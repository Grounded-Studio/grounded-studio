Why You Get a Notification Before Your Food Expires (And How to Time It Right)

📅 July 27, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read ✍️ Grounded Studio

Almost every food waste story ends the same way: "I forgot it was there." The yoghurt at the back of the shelf. The half-open pack of chorizo. The bag of spinach behind the ketchup. Nobody sets out to throw food away — it just quietly expires while your attention is somewhere else.

Notifications solve exactly this problem, but only if they're timed properly. Too early and you ignore them; too late and the food is already gone. Below is how FreshGarant handles it — and how you can adjust the timing to match how your household actually eats.

Why "one day before" isn't enough

The instinct is to set expiry notifications for one day before. It sounds sensible: "warn me the day before it goes off." In practice it's the worst possible setting. You get the notification at lunchtime on Thursday, the yoghurt expires Friday, and you already have Thursday dinner planned. There's no time to actually use the food.

Real usage needs planning space. That's why FreshGarant's default sits at 3 days before expiry — enough runway to slot the item into that evening's meal, tomorrow's lunch, or Friday's leftovers night. Not so far ahead that it feels irrelevant.

The recommended defaults

Fresh produce & dairy
3 days before — enough time to plan a meal around it, or to freeze if you can't.
Meat & fish
2 days before — shorter runway, but critical to catch before it turns.
Bread & bakery
2 days before — enough time to freeze slices you won't finish.
Dry goods & preserves
7 days before — long shelf life means longer warning is fine, less urgent.

The morning slot vs. the evening slot

Notifications also depend on when in the day they fire. Two windows work well; the rest are noise:

  • 08:00–09:00 (morning slot) — you're planning lunch and dinner, so an expiry alert can actually shape your day. Best for households where someone cooks at home.
  • 17:00–18:00 (pre-dinner slot) — you're deciding what to make. An expiry ping at this moment is basically a suggestion. Best for households where dinner decisions happen last-minute.

Firing at 22:00 or during the workday tends to get dismissed and forgotten. FreshGarant defaults to the morning slot but you can shift it in settings.

The "one notification per day" rule

If five items are expiring in the next three days, you don't want five separate notifications. That's how apps get muted. FreshGarant bundles them into a single daily digest: "3 items expire this week — spinach (Tue), yoghurt (Wed), pesto (Thu)". One tap opens the list, one glance tells you what to plan around. That's it.

Free tip: keep an "eat soon" shelf in your fridge

Regardless of any app: designate the front-left corner of the fridge as your "eat soon" zone. When you add a product with a short expiry, put it there. When you decide what to cook, check that shelf first. This one physical habit does what notifications do — it makes expiring items visible — and takes zero configuration.

The permission problem (and why we ask later)

Since Android 13, apps have to ask permission before sending notifications. Most apps ask on the second screen of onboarding — which is exactly when users have no idea whether they want notifications yet, so they deny by default.

FreshGarant asks after you've added your first product. At that point the value is obvious: "you just told me your milk expires Friday — want me to remind you Wednesday?" Acceptance rate is roughly 4x higher than the ask-on-launch approach, and users are happier because they understand what they're saying yes to.

How to change your settings

  1. Open FreshGarant → tap the ⚙️ settings icon.
  2. Choose Notifications.
  3. Adjust Warning window (1–7 days).
  4. Choose your preferred time (morning or evening).
  5. Optional: enable quiet weekends if you don't want alerts on Saturday/Sunday.

All settings are local — nothing leaves your device. The app calculates upcoming expiries on your phone and schedules notifications through Android's own reminder system.

The one setting most users get wrong

People often crank the warning window up to 7 days for everything, thinking "more warning = safer." It's the opposite. At 7 days, the reminder feels premature — you dismiss it, and by the time expiry actually comes, you've forgotten the reminder ever fired.

The sweet spot for most users is 3 days for perishables, 2 days for meat/fish, 7 days only for dry goods. This matches how you actually plan meals — a few days out, not a week — and stops the notifications from turning into background noise.

Notifications aren't the goal

An honest thing to say about an app whose main feature is reminders: the goal isn't to send lots of them. It's to send one at the right moment. Everything else — the barcode scanner, the AI photo tool, the price tracker — feeds into that one moment. When it works, you eat the yoghurt instead of throwing it away. That's the whole loop.
